## PMID 40833744 — The Impact of Concordance between Liquid and Tissue Biopsy for Actionable Mutations: Insights from the ROME Trial

**Identity and provenance.** Issue year 2026; supplied version `publisher_typeset_pdf`; SHA-256 `9cfaa4454cacbfe52b1986cf386a2da557d87b28840b51a533d6c5c4e957d272`; 11 physical pages. Supplement status is `not_supplied` and no supplement was reviewed.

**Coverage.** Abstract/Introduction: pp. 1–2; Materials and Methods: pp. 2–3; Results: pp. 3–6; Discussion: pp. 6–9; references and end matter: pp. 9–11. Main visuals checked with their captions: Fig. 1 (p. 4), Table 1 (p. 5), Table 2 (p. 6), Fig. 2 (p. 7), and Figs. 3–4 (p. 8). All were legible.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** This is a retrospective, exploratory biomarker analysis nested in the randomized phase II ROME trial. Among 400 randomized patients (200 tissue-agnostic targeted therapy [TT], 200 standard of care [SoC]), concordance meant the same actionable alteration in tissue and liquid biopsy. There were 197 concordant patients (95 TT, 102 SoC) and 203 discordant patients (139 tissue-only, 64 liquid-only); the discordant category included assay failures as well as true discordance. Randomization supports TT-versus-SoC comparisons within a defined stratum, but the concordant-versus-discordant comparison itself was not randomized.

**Conclusion-driving results.** In the concordant stratum, TT was associated with longer PFS than SoC (4.90 vs 2.80 months; HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.41–0.77) and a numerically higher ORR (22.1% vs 12.8%); the OS comparison was not statistically significant (10.89 vs 7.70 months; HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.53–1.09). In tissue-only patients, neither OS (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.73–1.78) nor PFS (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.56–1.17) was significant. In liquid-only patients, neither OS (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.63–1.92) nor PFS (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.46–1.32) was significant. The true-discordant analysis was likewise null for OS (HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.81–1.79) and PFS (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.62–1.23). The authors state that thresholds and power calculations were not prespecified; subgroup interaction evidence was not demonstrated. Safety was not a conclusion-driving endpoint of this concordance analysis, so no new safety claim is transferable.

**Source alerts and disposition.** The abstract reports slightly different concordant-stratum estimates (OS 11.05 months, HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.51–1.07; PFS 4.93 months, HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.40–0.76) from the Results/Fig. 2 values above. Fig. 2's caption also labels the experimental group as `n=85`, whereas the flow diagram, Table 1, text, and risk table support `n=95`. These conflicts are documented; exact abstract estimates and the caption denominator are withheld from reusable language. The strongest defensible claim is that dual-biopsy concordance identified an exploratory subgroup with a larger TT PFS signal; it does not establish concordance as a predictive biomarker or prove that dual testing improves survival.

**Language samples and transfer.**

- Methods, p. 3, limitation-first move: the analysis explicitly notes its exploratory nature and absence of prespecified thresholds/power. Safe frame: “Given the exploratory design, [thresholds/power calculations] were not prespecified, and the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.”
- Results, p. 5, bounded comparison: the authors use “experienced less benefit” rather than declaring no efficacy in discordant groups. Safe frame: “Compared with [reference group], [subgroup] experienced less apparent benefit from [intervention], although [interaction/confirmatory evidence] was not established.”
- Discussion, pp. 8–9, association-to-validation move: concordance is presented as a potential selection signal followed by a prospective-validation requirement. Safe frame: “The observed association supports prospective evaluation of [biomarker] as a candidate stratification factor.”

Unsafe transfer: “Concordant tissue and liquid biopsy predicts targeted-therapy benefit and improves survival.” This converts an exploratory subgroup pattern into validated prediction and a causal testing benefit.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** The study assessed a tumor-informed whole-genome ctDNA assay in 202 immunotherapy-treated patients across 24 solid-tumor types and 1,455 longitudinal samples: a retrospective primary cohort (`n=136`) and an independent prospectively collected validation cohort (`n=66`). Up to roughly 1,800 tumor-specific variants were followed; median reported limit of detection was 1.9 parts per million and baseline ctDNA was detected in 199/202 patients. Molecular response (mR) was defined as at least a 30% decline before cycle 2, a threshold selected for model fit in the primary cohort.

**Conclusion-driving results.** Failure to achieve mR was associated with shorter PFS (HR 2.69, 95% CI 1.72–4.21) and OS (HR 2.23, 95% CI 1.41–3.54) in the primary cohort and remained associated in validation (PFS HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.06–4.33; OS HR 2.56, 95% CI 1.17–5.59). Molecular complete response was uncommon (14 primary-cohort patients); durable mCR was rarer (eight patients) and could not be independently validated because the validation cohort had only two mCRs and one durable mCR. In immune-unconfirmed progression, iPFS differed from conventional PFS (`P=.02`), but additional stratification by ctDNA dynamics was not significant (`P=.09`); a single pseudoprogression case cannot validate discrimination. Model C-indices were 0.68/0.71 for PFS/OS in the primary cohort and 0.66/0.69 in validation. Safety and treatment toxicity were not evaluated as assay-driven outcomes.

**Source alerts and disposition.** For durable mCR OS, Fig. 3 shows `P=3.03×10^-5`, whereas the Results text reports `3.03×10^-6`; the exact p value is withheld. Fig. 5's caption misdescribes panels D/E/I/J as ctDNA-mCR panels although they display cluster analyses. The Fig. 5 text and rendered panels reverse the validation PFS/OS p-value labels (`.01` and `.03`); those exact p values are not reused. The assay association does not demonstrate clinical utility of ctDNA-guided treatment, and small heterogeneous phase I cohorts, tissue/turnaround requirements, a data-derived threshold, and very small response subgroups constrain generalization.

- Discussion, p. 14, scope-limiting contrast: the article acknowledges retrospective analysis, cohort heterogeneity, and tissue requirements before proposing future use. Safe frame: “Although [signal] was reproduced in an independent cohort, [design and implementation limitations] preclude claims of treatment-guiding utility.”

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** Using matched tumor-normal data from the Japanese C-CAT clinicogenomic database, the authors developed variant-level models to estimate whether a pathogenic variant seen on tumor-only sequencing was germline. The main text identifies 7,048 discovery patients and a modeling set of 2,297 patients/3,317 variants. Predictors included age, multiple cancers, gene, organ, population allele frequency, age–gene terms, relative VAF, and tumor allele ratio. A separate GenMineTOP panel dataset was used for validation, although it came from the same national C-CAT ecosystem; VAF was unavailable there and was fixed/imputed.

**Conclusion-driving results.** In development/internal validation, the nomogram, LightGBM, and random forest showed AUCs/c-indices of approximately 0.961, 0.97, and 0.96, outperforming ESMO (`0.85`) and Kosugi (`0.88`) rule-based criteria. In the independent panel dataset, all three models had reported AUCs near 0.97 versus approximately 0.90 for ESMO. These are discrimination results for triage; they do not establish that tumor-only sequencing diagnoses germline status. Calibration, missing panel-undetected variants, structural variants, limited oncogene representation, and Japanese-only development constrain use; confirmatory germline testing remains necessary. Safety is not applicable.

**Source alerts and disposition.** The abstract says 7,078 cases, whereas Methods/Results say 7,048; the main-text denominator is retained and the abstract value is withheld. The stated total of 4,905 variants is inconsistent with its listed components (4,410 somatic + 491 germline + 8 CHIP = 4,909), so those component totals are not propagated as a reconciled total. Validation text refers both to 1,223 variants and to application in 2,089 variants (2,018 somatic, 71 germline), requiring denominator qualification. Net-benefit values and the abstract's comparator AUC also vary from Results/Fig. 5, so exact decision-curve benefit is withheld. The high AUC is reusable only as variant-level discrimination in the reported Japanese datasets.

- Discussion, pp. 9–10, implementation boundary: high discrimination is followed by limitations and a confirmatory-testing requirement. Safe frame: “The model may support prioritization for [confirmatory procedure], but it should not replace [reference-standard test].”

**Conclusion-driving results.** In the primary TMB ≥16 population, ORR was 22.3% (95% CI 15.0%–31.2%), with the lower confidence bound exceeding the prespecified 10% historical threshold. **Question, design, and analysis sets.** This was an exploratory MRD analysis within randomized NADIM II, using a tumor-agnostic, methylation-enabled Guardant Reveal assay after neoadjuvant treatment and surgery. Sixty of 73 resected patients were MRD-evaluable (45 experimental, 15 control). Eighty-six samples were assayed and 83 were valid; MRD was detected in eight valid samples from seven patients. MRD status was analyzed at any sampled postoperative time rather than through a described time-dependent Cox model.

**Conclusion-driving results.** MRD positivity at any time was associated with shorter EFS (HR 10.20, 95% CI 3.68–28.26; `P<.001`) and OS (HR 9.99, 95% CI 2.00–49.92; `P=.01`), with wide intervals. **Question, design, and analysis sets.** This ongoing open-label, multicenter, first-in-human phase I study enrolled 86 patients with heterogeneous advanced solid tumors across 2.25–2,500 mg every three weeks: 61 in exploration/backfill and 25 in a 2,000-mg expansion. Seventy-eight patients were immunotherapy-naive. Safety included all treated patients; DLT analysis used its protocol-defined evaluable set. Paired tumor multi-omics/pharmacodynamic subsets were much smaller than the treated cohort.

**Conclusion-driving results.** No DLT occurred through 1,500 mg in the initial 26 patients; DLTs occurred in one of four patients at 2,000 mg and one of seven at 2,500 mg. Table 3 calls the population “modified response-evaluable/efficacy analysis set,” while the text says all 86 were response-evaluable although eight were listed as not evaluable; use the table's qualified label. Historical pharmacodynamic and efficacy comparisons cannot establish superiority.

**Conclusion-driving clinical results.** In the retrospective cohort, the 1–3 Gy group had higher ORR (48.2% vs 20.6% for <1 Gy and 6.0% for >3 Gy), median PFS 10.2 versus 7.0 and 3.7 months, and median OS 22.8 versus 13.4 and 7.7 months. Relative to 1–3 Gy, adjusted OS was worse for >3 Gy (HR 4.87, 95% CI 3.37–7.02) and <1 Gy (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.29–2.66); observational adjustment cannot remove unmeasured confounding. In the prospective cohort (`n=10`, `11`, and `9` across dose strata), ORR was numerically highest at 1–3 Gy (50.0%) but not significant (`P=.323`); DCR differed (`90.0%`, `63.7%`, `33.3%; P=.041`), and 1–3 Gy versus >3 Gy PFS was reported as `P=.046`. Safety events were mainly grade 1–2; grade ≥3 cytopenias occurred, no grade ≥3 immune-related AE was observed, and no increased intestinal toxicity was seen at 1–3 Gy. With only ten patients and 12.4 months median follow-up, long-term safety is not established.

**Multi-omics and negative results.** PR patients already had greater observed-species diversity before radiotherapy (`P=.041`) and after (`P=.017`); Shannon-index differences were only trends (`P=.087` and `.098`). Beta diversity differed before radiotherapy (`P=.027`) but not after (`P=.387`), weakening a treatment-induced interpretation. LEfSe taxa and untargeted metabolites were exploratory and largely based on nominal p values without a stated study-wide multiplicity correction. Indole-pathway enrichment was not significant. Within the 1–3 Gy group, circulating α4β7+ Tregs decreased at a borderline nominal `P=.049`; RORγt+ α4β7+ Tregs did not change significantly. MIP-3α increased only as a trend within that group (`P=.061`), although post-radiotherapy levels differed across groups in supplementary analyses. Correlations between Tregs and indole metabolites were nonsignificant before (`P=.619`) and after (`P=.132`) irradiation. Routine immune-cell counts and several cytokines showed no significant dose-group changes.

**Inference boundary and source alerts.** The title and parts of the abstract/discussion use causal verbs (“enhances,” “improves”), but both cohorts were observational and nonrandomized. The strongest defensible claim is an association between a 1–3 Gy SIMRD window and better outcomes, accompanied by hypothesis-generating microbiome, metabolite, and immune correlates that require randomized prospective validation. Baseline PR/non-PR omic differences prevent attribution to irradiation, and the dose group may encode target location, treatment volume, disease burden, or other care differences. Table 1 labels the non-squamous category simply “NSCLC,” whereas Fig. 2 uses “non-squamous cell carcinoma”; transfer should use the latter interpretation only with qualification. “Increased MIP-3α” must not replace the nonsignificant within-group trend.
