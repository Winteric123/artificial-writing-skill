### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/population: this was a multicenter, open-label, first-in-human phase I monotherapy study of the SEZ6-targeting calicheamicin antibody-drug conjugate ABBV-011 in relapsed or refractory SCLC. Dose escalation evaluated 0.3-2.0 mg/kg every three weeks, a Japanese evaluation included three patients, and expansion required archival or recent tumor SEZ6 expression in at least 25% of tumor cells at intensity 1+ or greater. There was no randomized or contemporaneous control group.
- Analysis sets and denominators: 99 patients received at least one dose and formed the safety population: dose escalation n=36, Japanese evaluation n=3, and expansion n=60. The 1-mg/kg expansion cohort contained 40 treated patients. Table 3 used 98 patients for the all-dose efficacy analysis because one patient at 0.8 mg/kg had received one dose and had no postbaseline assessment; the Methods state that other patients lacking postbaseline assessment were counted as nonresponders. Figure 1 instead shows measurable postbaseline target-lesion change for n=90 overall and n=38 at 1 mg/kg. These are distinct safety, response, and waterfall populations and must not be merged.
- Dose-finding boundary: one of nine patients at 2 mg/kg had a dose-limiting grade 3 fatigue event during the prespecified DLT window, and a formal MTD was not identified. Nevertheless, cumulative delayed hepatotoxicity after the DLT window prompted successive reductions from 2 mg/kg to 1.6 mg/kg in cycle 1/1.2 mg/kg thereafter and then to 1 mg/kg. The 0.8-mg/kg cohort was still being explored, and the Discussion states that the RP2D remained to be confirmed. Therefore, “MTD not reached” cannot be used to imply absence of dose-limiting cumulative toxicity or a settled optimal dose.
- Overall antitumor activity: among 98 patients in Table 3, confirmed ORR was 19/98 (19%; 95% CI, 12%-29%), including one complete response and 18 partial responses. Clinical benefit rate was 68/98 (69%; 95% CI, 59%-78%), and benefit lasting more than 12 weeks was 36/98 (37%; 95% CI, 27%-47%). Median DOR was 3.5 months (95% CI, 2.8-4.7), and median duration of clinical benefit was 5.6 months (95% CI, 4.6-6.7).
- Activity at the preliminary 1-mg/kg dose: in the 40-patient expansion cohort, confirmed ORR was 10/40 (25%; 95% CI, 13%-41%), clinical benefit rate 26/40 (65%; 95% CI, 48%-79%), and benefit lasting more than 12 weeks 17/40 (43%; 95% CI, 27%-59%). Median DOR was 4.2 months (95% CI, 2.6-6.7), median duration of clinical benefit 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.1-7.6), and median PFS 3.5 months (95% CI, 1.5-4.2). Median PFS was 5.4 months (95% CI, 1.4-8.3) in the one-prior-line subgroup (n=12) and 2.7 months (95% CI, 1.4-4.1) with two or more prior lines (n=28); these small nonrandomized strata are descriptive rather than comparative treatment-effect estimates.
- SEZ6 prevalence and predictive uncertainty: among 445 prescreened, screened, or enrolled SCLC specimens, 86% had at least 1% tumor cells with 1+ or greater staining and 55% met the expansion cutoff of at least 25%. These prevalence data span a broader specimen pool than the treated efficacy cohort. Preliminary expansion analyses did not show a correlation between objective response and SEZ6 expression level. Most biomarker specimens were archival and collected before intervening chemotherapy or immunotherapy, so the study neither validates the cutoff as predictive nor establishes stability of SEZ6 over time.
- Safety: among 99 treated patients, 96 (97%) had a TEAE, 63 (64%) a grade 3 or higher TEAE, 76 (77%) a treatment-related AE, 34 (34%) a grade 3 or higher treatment-related AE, 41 (41%) a serious TEAE, and six (6%) a serious treatment-related AE. TEAEs led to discontinuation in 19%, interruption in 34%, and reduction in 8%; treatment-related AEs led to discontinuation in 13%, interruption in 29%, and reduction in 8%. Nineteen patients (19%) had a TEAE leading to death, attributed to progressive disease in 16 and to stroke, cardiac failure, and respiratory distress in one each; none was judged treatment related.
- Hepatic safety: hepatotoxic TEAEs occurred in 42/99 (42%), including grade 3 or higher events in 12/99 (12%). Two patients had grade 3 veno-occlusive disease, one after a single 1.6-mg/kg dose and one after three 1-mg/kg doses; both resolved with treatment. Delayed GGT and bilirubin elevations had median onsets around 50-65 days in the dose-decision cohorts and were central to dose reduction. The absence of grade greater than 2 neurologic events and treatment-related ocular toxicity does not offset the observed hepatic burden.
- Positive result: ABBV-011 produced confirmed responses and short-term disease control in a molecularly selected, heavily pretreated single-arm SCLC cohort, with a 25% ORR at the preliminary 1-mg/kg expansion dose.
- Negative/uncertain result: there was no control arm; DOR and PFS were imprecise; the biomarker-response correlation was not demonstrated; RP2D was not finalized; and cumulative delayed hepatic toxicity was clinically important despite the formal MTD not being reached.
- Claim ceiling: the source supports preliminary single-arm activity and a dose/safety characterization for ABBV-011 in SEZ6-selected relapsed/refractory SCLC. It does not establish comparative efficacy, survival benefit, a validated predictive SEZ6 threshold, absence of serious cumulative toxicity, definitive RP2D, or favorable benefit-risk relative to another therapy.

### Language and transfer

- Methods/analysis-set reporting (pp. 2-3): the source distinguishes the treated safety population, response-evaluable rules, and dose-specific expansion population. Reusable logic: name each analysis set before its estimate and state how patients without postbaseline assessment were handled.
- Results/benefit-risk pairing (pp. 4-8): activity estimates are accompanied by delayed hepatotoxicity, VOD, dose modifications, and the unresolved RP2D. Reusable logic: report the efficacy signal and the toxicity that altered dose selection in the same evidentiary frame.
- Results/negative biomarker finding (p. 8): broad expression and a prespecified enrollment cutoff are followed by a preliminary absence of response correlation. Reusable logic: separate target prevalence from predictive validation and retain an explicit negative correlative result.
- Discussion/calibration (p. 8): the paper acknowledges modest sample size, preselection, lack of a control arm, archival biomarker tissue, and the need for randomized confirmation. Reusable logic: use “preliminary” or “encouraging” only with the exact design and confirmatory need visible.
- Safe transfer template: `In this single-arm phase I cohort, [agent] produced a confirmed response rate of [estimate; CI] in [exact analysis set], while delayed [toxicity] prompted dose modification and [biomarker] was not correlated with response; these findings support preliminary activity rather than comparative efficacy or biomarker validation.`
- Unsafe transfer: combining n=99, n=98, n=90, n=40, and n=38; describing “MTD not reached” as proof of a settled safe dose; omitting grade 3 VOD or delayed hepatotoxicity from “well tolerated” language; calling SEZ6 expression a validated response predictor; or claiming survival benefit or superiority from cross-trial comparisons.

- Year: 2024.

### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/model chain: this is an integrated translational and preclinical study asking whether YAP1 stratifies pulmonary LCNEC into biologically distinct subtypes and suggests subtype-linked vulnerabilities. Evidence layers include CLIA-certified IHC in patient tumors, bulk tumor and cell-line transcriptomics/proteomics, CCLE genomic and drug-dependency data, six relapsed patient biopsies and six PDX models profiled by single-cell RNA sequencing, perturbation studies, two xenograft models, surface-protein flow cytometry, and in-vitro CAR-T assays. It contains no biomarker-stratified therapeutic patient cohort.
- Clinical specimens and subtype observation: YAP1, ASCL1, NEUROD1, and POU2F3 IHC was assessed in 78 LCNEC and 18 SCLC tumors. Nuclear YAP1 was present in approximately half of LCNEC tumors. YAP1 H-scores did not differ between treatment-naive and previously treated LCNEC (Figure 1C, P=0.441), and relapse-state comparisons in SCLC or mixed histology were underpowered. This negative result prevents interpreting YAP1 status as a treatment-acquired state from these data.
- Genomic layer: WGS data from 21 LCNEC cell lines showed CDKN2A/B homozygous deletions in 67%/47% and SMARCA4 mutations in 40% of YAP1-high lines, versus none of those alterations in the YAP1-low group. Intact RB1 was more common in YAP1-high than YAP1-low lines (87% vs 50%), while TP53 mutation frequency did not differ. Patient genomic observations were described as small and located in an unavailable supplementary table, so cell-line frequencies must not be represented as patient prevalence.
- Bulk molecular phenotype: YAP1-high tumors and models showed higher YAP/TAZ and EMT-related programs, lower neuroendocrine programs, and a relatively inflamed phenotype. Relevant Figure 2 results included lower p16 in LCNEC than SCLC cells (P<0.001; fold change -2.7), higher RB1 (P<0.001; fold change 3.1), a nonsignificant p16 difference within LCNEC YAP1 strata (P=0.07; fold change -1.3), higher patient-tumor RB1 in YAP1-high disease (P=0.008; fold change 1.5), positive RB1-YAP1 correlation (P=0.004; r=0.35), and negative CDKN2A-YAP1 (P=0.003; r=-0.33) and SMARCA4-YAP1 (P=0.008; r=-0.32) correlations. These are cross-sectional associations except where a specific perturbation was performed.
- Immune inference boundary: CIBERSORTx inferred greater absolute immune infiltrate in YAP1-high bulk tumors (Figure 3F, P=0.004); CD274 expression was also higher (P=0.007; fold change 2.03), and YAP1 overexpression increased PD-L1 in one YAP1-low cell line. These molecular and inferred-immune findings do not show that YAP1 predicts immune-checkpoint-inhibitor benefit because no treated outcome comparison or treatment interaction was tested.
- Single-cell layer and analytic unit: six relapsed LCNEC/combined-histology biopsies yielded 18,378 QC-passing cells, including 8,069 pooled cancer cells; six PDX tumors yielded 15,246 cells. YAP1-positive populations had higher YAP/TAZ/EMT programs, and YAP1-high biopsies showed higher proportions of macrophages, CD8 T cells, and NK cells, reported as P<0.001 for all immune populations. The independent patient sample remains six, however; large cell counts and multiple nested cell-population comparisons do not create thousands of independent tumors. The displayed analysis does not clearly document patient-clustered inference or a multiplicity strategy.
- Platinum and direct YAP1-treatment negatives: YAP1 status did not predict cisplatin response in LCNEC cells (Figure 5A, P=0.19) or cell-line/PDX xenografts (Figure 5B, P=0.12). Verteporfin response was likewise not YAP1-specific (Figure 5D, P=0.71), and YAP1 overexpression did not change verteporfin response. Other YAP/TEAD agents mainly reduced proliferation independent of YAP1 status. These results directly limit treatment-prediction and direct-YAP1-dependency claims.
- Exploratory dependency and MEK evidence: an exploratory DepMap comparison of 15 YAP1-high versus six YAP1-low LCNEC lines nominated several agents without a stated correction for the broad drug screen. Trametinib delayed tumor growth in two YAP1-high xenografts, H1915 LCNEC (P=0.03) and SW1271 SCLC (P=0.01), but the main caption does not state group sizes, toxicity is not shown, and one model is SCLC rather than LCNEC. Combinations with cisplatin or palbociclib showed no additive effect. This supports a preclinical MEK-inhibitor hypothesis, not clinical treatment selection.
- Source locator conflict: the Results repeats the same YAP1-overexpression/trametinib statement while citing Supplementary Figure S6e on one occasion and Supplementary Figure S4d on another. Because the supplement was not supplied, the intended locator cannot be resolved and neither citation should be silently preferred.
- Surfaceome and CAR-T layer: patient-tumor mRNA showed higher AXL in YAP1-high disease (P<0.001; fold change 2.25) and lower DLL3 and NCAM1 (both P<0.001; fold changes -6.22 and -4.88). Flow cytometry supported higher cell-surface AXL (P=0.02; fold change 23.14), but cell-surface DLL3 (P=0.72) and CD56 (P=0.31) did not differ significantly between YAP1 strata. This transcript-surface discordance is a necessary boundary. In selected cell lines, DLL3/CD56 CAR-T cytotoxicity favored YAP1-low models and AXL CAR-T favored YAP1-high models; AXL abundance correlated with AXL CAR-T cytotoxicity (P=0.02; Spearman rho=0.83). These are limited in-vitro assays, not animal or patient CAR-T efficacy.
- Heterogeneity: single-cell data showed that AXL and DLL3 or NCAM1 were largely expressed by mutually exclusive cell populations even within an individual biopsy. Thus, a bulk subtype label does not imply uniform target expression in every tumor cell.
- Safety, prognosis, and response: the paper provides no patient treatment-response, survival/prognostic validation, or clinical safety analysis.
- Positive result: convergent IHC, bulk, single-cell, perturbational, and preclinical model data support a YAP1-associated LCNEC taxonomy with distinct genomic, neuroendocrine/mesenchymal, immune, and surface-target features.
- Negative/uncertain result: YAP1 did not distinguish treatment-naive from treated tumors, did not predict cisplatin response, did not define verteporfin sensitivity, and did not yield additive cisplatin/palbociclib combinations. Immune and surface-target hypotheses lack clinical outcome validation, and several analyses have small independent sample sizes or broad exploratory testing.
- Claim ceiling: the source supports a biological and preclinical LCNEC subtype framework and candidate therapeutic hypotheses. It does not establish YAP1 as a prognostic or predictive clinical biomarker, demonstrate immune-checkpoint benefit, validate MEK inhibition or CAR-T therapy in patients, prove uniform target expression, or justify treatment selection outside prospective validation.

### Language and transfer

- Methods/multilayer specification (pp. 3-6): the source moves across IHC, bulk omics, single-cell data, dependency screens, xenografts, flow cytometry, and CAR-T assays. Reusable logic: identify the data layer and independent experimental unit every time rather than merging them into a generic “multi-omics cohort.”
- Results/subtype description (pp. 6-10): genomic and transcriptomic associations are paired with perturbational observations. Reusable logic: reserve causal language for the individual knockdown/overexpression experiment and use association language for cross-sectional patient data.
- Results/negative therapeutic findings (pp. 10-11 and Figure 5): cisplatin, verteporfin, and combination negatives precede the trametinib signal. Reusable logic: report the failed direct or standard-treatment hypotheses before presenting an exploratory vulnerability.
- Results/cross-modality discordance (pp. 11-12 and Figure 6): tumor mRNA separation for DLL3/NCAM1 was not reproduced as a significant cell-surface difference in cell lines. Reusable logic: distinguish transcript abundance from accessible surface antigen and preserve discordant assays.
- Discussion/clinical ceiling (pp. 12-13): the proposed clinical distinctions are prospective hypotheses because biomarker-driven outcome data are absent. Reusable logic: use “supports evaluation” rather than “predicts benefit.”
- Safe transfer template: `Across [specified tissue/model layers], [marker]-high tumors were associated with [molecular phenotype], whereas [negative assay/treatment result] was not different; the small independent sample size and absence of a treated clinical cohort limit the finding to a subtype and therapeutic-hypothesis framework.`
- Unsafe transfer: calling YAP1 prognostic or predictive; inferring immune-checkpoint response from TIS/PD-L1/CIBERSORTx; using cell counts as the patient sample size; equating transcript abundance with surface antigen; claiming direct YAP1 inhibition works when the tested agents were nonspecific; treating two xenografts or in-vitro CAR-T assays as patient efficacy; or silently choosing between Supplementary Figures S6e and S4d.

## PMID 39150541

### Identity and coverage

- Title: *Transcriptomic Heterogeneity of EGFR-Mutant Non–Small Cell Lung Cancer Evolution Toward Small-Cell Lung Cancer*.
- Year: 2024.
- Supplied version: publisher typeset PDF.
- Registered PDF: `Oh_EGFR_NSCLC_Evolution_to_SCLC_PMID39150541.pdf`.
- SHA-256: `9f3bf2507ab3e8b6afecbfc55b6d011dd70bb0f9bb6b812b11e664f9183c1529`.
- Physical pages: 14/14 visually reviewed; no unreadable page.
- Supplement status: `not_supplied`. Supplementary Tables S1-S7 and Supplementary Figures S1-S7 were referenced but not available for review.

### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/population: this retrospective translational study examined transcriptomic heterogeneity during transformation from EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma or combined histology to treatment-resistant SCLC and explored subtype-linked preclinical therapeutic strategies. The cohort comprised 11 patients diagnosed from 2012-2021: eight with EGFR-mutant LADC or combined SCLC/LADC that progressed to EGFR-TKI-resistant SCLC and three with EGFR-wild-type combined SCLC/LADC. It is not a prospective treatment trial.
- Clinical context: the eight EGFR-mutant cases included three L858R and five exon 19 deletion tumors. Median time to SCLC transformation was 18.1 months (range, 0-50.4), and reported median PFS on etoposide/platinum was 5.9 months (range, 0.7-22.9), but the patient-level clinical detail was supplement-dependent and cannot be fully audited here.
- Spatial analysis set: ten patients contributed 59 GeoMx regions of interest: 22 LADC, 30 SCLC, and seven CSCLC ROIs; 17 ROIs were EGFR wild type. Approximately 14,000 genes were measured. ROIs are nested within patients, so 59 regions cannot be treated as 59 independent patients. The displayed statistical workflow does not establish patient-clustered inference or a comprehensive multiplicity procedure.
- Subtype findings and source ambiguity: all transformed-SCLC ROIs expressed at least one of ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, or YAP1, with substantial intratumoral heterogeneity. The Results states that 66.6% of “CSCLC samples” before EGFR-TKI were non-neuroendocrine (14/21 ROIs), whereas 93.7% of post-EGFR-TKI transformed-SCLC samples were neuroendocrine-high (15/16 ROIs). However, the cohort map had identified only seven CSCLC ROIs in total. The source apparently uses “CSCLC samples” differently in these contexts, but the exact reconciliation is not provided. The 14/21 and 15/16 result may be reported only with this terminology/denominator alert, not silently mapped onto the seven-ROI category.
- Subtype assay discordance: IHC and mRNA-based subtype assignment disagreed in 5/12 cases (41.6%). This directly limits substituting one assay for the other or claiming a clinically settled subtype classification.
- EGFR and lineage-state result: EGFR expression was lower in SCLC than LADC/combined components (Figure 3; P<0.001). Figure 3C used 19 treatment-naive LADC, seven CSCLC, and 11 SCLC ROIs; Figure 3D used all 22, seven, and 30 ROIs. The reduction was observed across lineage states and was not restricted to EGFR-mutant or post-TKI material, so the data show an association with SCLC phenotype rather than proving that TKI exposure caused EGFR loss.
- Transcriptomic/omics result: differential-expression analyses indicated reduced RTK and immune-related programs and increased neuronal and epigenetic/chromatin-modifier programs during transformation. The authors explicitly note the rarity of paired samples and lack of multi-omics validation. Spatial transcriptomics alone does not establish protein activity, causal direction, or a validated therapeutic biomarker.
- BET-inhibitor evidence and human anecdote: the NEUROD1/MYC-amplified SNU-2962A model was sensitive to JQ1 (reported IC50 136.45 nmol/L), with apoptotic changes. In contrast, patient LC02, classified as ASCL1, received molibresib and had PFS of 1.2 months. This single patient does not establish subtype-specific clinical efficacy and, if mentioned, is a limited/negative clinical anecdote rather than validation.
- HDAC/EGFR restoration and crucial replication negative: fimepinostat restored EGFR expression and combined synergistically with osimertinib in SNU-2962A 2D cells. In the second transformed-SCLC line, SNU-4505, fimepinostat did not significantly increase EGFR and no synergistic viability effect was observed. This failure to reproduce the central mechanism in a second line must accompany any general statement about epigenetic resensitization.
- Organoid result: an organoid derived from SNU-2962A remained resistant to osimertinib alone; fimepinostat had an IC50 of 7.682 nmol/L (R-squared=0.9585), restored EGFR/p-EGFR staining, and combined with osimertinib with a combination index below one. Because the organoid derives from the same patient/model as the positive 2D system, it is cross-format validation, not an independent-patient replication.
- Xenograft result: SNU-2962A xenografts were randomized to vehicle, osimertinib 25 mg/kg daily, fimepinostat 10 mg/kg on a five-days-on/two-days-off schedule, or the combination, with n=5 per group. The combination delayed tumor growth relative to individual agents (reported P<0.001), while the plotted single agents did not provide a comparable effect. This is a small, immunodeficient, single-model preclinical result without a patient efficacy or safety endpoint.
- Mechanistic wording alert: the Results describes increased “PARP molecules” as evidence of apoptotic signaling; the blot label and text do not justify silently rewriting this as a specific cleaved-PARP result. Safer transfer is “changes in apoptosis-related proteins/signaling.”
- Safety, prognosis, and prediction: apart from the limited treatment history and one molibresib anecdote, there is no prospective patient efficacy, comparative safety, prognostic validation, or treatment-interaction analysis.
- Positive result: spatial transcriptomics revealed heterogeneous SCLC subtype programs and lineage-associated EGFR/pathway changes, while one patient-derived NEUROD1 model showed reproducible 2D/organoid/xenograft evidence that fimepinostat could restore EGFR and enhance osimertinib activity.
- Negative/uncertain result: IHC and mRNA subtype assignments were frequently discordant; the SNU-4505 line did not reproduce EGFR restoration or synergy; molibresib PFS was short in one ASCL1 patient; and the source contains a category/denominator ambiguity for “CSCLC samples.”
- Claim ceiling: the source supports a spatial-transcriptomic description of transformed-SCLC heterogeneity and a model-specific preclinical resensitization hypothesis. It does not show that EGFR-TKI exposure causes the observed lineage program, establish a clinical subtype assay, validate fimepinostat/osimertinib or BET inhibition in patients, or support efficacy, prognosis, or safety claims.

### Language and transfer

- Methods/spatial denominator (pp. 2-5): the source distinguishes patients, specimens, and nested ROIs. Reusable logic: state the patient n before ROI n and avoid treating regions as independent clinical observations.
- Results/dynamic association (pp. 5-9): pre/post-TKI and LADC/SCLC contrasts are described alongside baseline combined-histology observations. Reusable logic: write “associated with lineage state” unless temporal sampling and causal design justify “induced by treatment.”
- Results/cross-assay heterogeneity (pp. 6-7): mRNA subtype calls and protein IHC disagree in 5/12 cases. Reusable logic: quantify discordance before proposing assay interchangeability.
- Results/preclinical triangulation (pp. 9-12): 2D, organoid, and xenograft evidence is strongest within SNU-2962A, but SNU-4505 is negative. Reusable logic: name the positive model lineage and the failed replication in the same paragraph.
- Discussion/evidence ceiling (pp. 12-13): the authors acknowledge rare paired samples and absent multi-omics validation. Reusable logic: present the treatment concept as a strategy for further study, not as a patient recommendation.
- Safe transfer template: `Spatial profiling of [patients/ROIs] associated [lineage state] with [transcriptomic feature], and [intervention] restored [target] and enhanced [drug] activity in one patient-derived model; failure to reproduce the effect in [second model] and the lack of clinical testing restrict the conclusion to a model-specific preclinical hypothesis.`

### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/model chain: this is a preclinical mechanism study asking whether EIF4A inhibition limits osteosarcoma lung metastasis by blocking stress-induced NRF2 synthesis. The evidence chain includes public human expression/outcome data, small institutional IHC series, multiple osteosarcoma cell lines, RNA sequencing and TMT proteomics, translation and reporter assays, inducible NFE2L2 knockdown, orthotopic and tail-vein mouse models, and an ex-vivo pulmonary metastasis assay. No osteosarcoma patient received CR-1-31B or zotatifin in this study.
- Human association layer: in TCGA sarcoma, high versus low EIF4A1 expression groups each contained 131 patients and showed worse overall survival for high expression (HR 2.1; log-rank P=0.00021; HR P=0.0003). This is a cross-sarcoma, unadjusted association, not osteosarcoma-specific prognostic validation. Institutional IHC compared 21 osteosarcoma specimens with 26 normal tissues (P<0.001); the survival subset contained only 17 patients, including four high and 13 medium/low EIF4A1 cases (log-rank P<0.05). The small, unadjusted subset does not establish an independent prognostic biomarker or treatment prediction.
- CR-1-31B primary-tumor model: in orthotopic MG63.3 xenografts, 0.2 mg/kg CR-1-31B reduced primary-tumor growth and prolonged time to endpoint versus vehicle (n=10/group; log-rank P<0.0001), without reported weight loss under that schedule. No spontaneous lung metastases occurred in this MG63.3 experiment, and tumors regrew after drug discontinuation; it therefore informs primary growth but not spontaneous-metastasis prevention.
- Metastasis models: in a tail-vein MG63.3 experimental-metastasis model, treatment beginning 21 days after injection reduced lung metastatic burden (n=12/group; P<0.0001). In an orthotopic MNNG model, CR-1-31B reduced spontaneous lung metastases (n=9/group; P<0.05), while also delaying primary-tumor growth, which can confound the amount of tumor available to disseminate. An ex-vivo pulmonary metastasis assay using MNNG cells showed less lung burden with 2 nmol/L CR-1-31B at day 14 (n=8/group; P<0.001). Tail-vein and ex-vivo assays support a colonization/outgrowth effect but do not reproduce the complete spontaneous metastatic cascade.
- Translation-dose boundary: low nanomolar CR-1-31B reduced proliferation at concentrations below those causing broad suppression in the puromycin assay. Nevertheless, at 4 nmol/L for 24 hours, polysome profiles showed greater 80S accumulation and reduced heavy polysomes. The defensible wording is selective or stress-sensitive translational inhibition at the studied low dose, not absolute absence of an effect on global translation.
- Omics layer: RNA-seq and TMT proteomics after 4 nmol/L CR-1-31B for 24 hours in MG63.3 cells highlighted oxidative-stress and NRF2-related programs. Transcriptomic pathway testing used adjusted P values, and proteomic filtering was prespecified, but both omics layers derive chiefly from one model and one exposure context. Pathway enrichment is hypothesis-generating and is not itself proof that NRF2 is the exclusive mediator.
- NRF2 mechanism: CR-1-31B sensitized osteosarcoma cells to tBHQ-induced oxidative stress, reduced spheroid growth, blocked induction of NRF2 protein, NQO1, and an ARE-mCherry reporter, and did not lower NFE2L2 mRNA; cotreatment could increase the transcript. NRF2 protein stability was not shortened. Metabolic labeling and polysome analyses instead supported reduced synthesis/translation of NRF2 under oxidative stress. These experiments support a translational mechanism more directly than a transcriptional or degradation mechanism.
- Genetic triangulation and incomplete mediation: inducible NFE2L2 knockdown reduced cell growth and pulmonary tumor burden and phenocopied part of the drug effect, but lung burden was not completely eliminated. Thus, NRF2 is supported as a relevant mediator, while other rocaglate-sensitive transcripts/pathways probably contribute.
- Zotatifin validation: the clinical-grade EIF4A inhibitor eFT226/zotatifin reproduced NRF2/ARE inhibition in vitro and reduced MG63.3 burden in the ex-vivo pulmonary assay at day 14 (n=8/group; P<0.001). The study did not test systemic zotatifin efficacy or safety in an osteosarcoma-bearing animal, and adult oncology trial development elsewhere is not osteosarcoma patient evidence.
- Source-internal inference tension: the Discussion states that CR-1-31B-mediated NRF2 inhibition is “necessary and sufficient” to inhibit lung colonization, yet the same passage acknowledges other downregulated proteins and explicitly notes that NFE2L2 knockdown reduced but did not eliminate lung burden. The stronger phrase is withheld. The supported conclusion is that NRF2 is a partial, biologically relevant mediator of the EIF4A-inhibitor phenotype.
- Safety: mouse body weight and observed tolerability are reported for selected regimens, but the study contains no human osteosarcoma safety profile, dose optimization, exposure-toxicity analysis, or comparison with standard chemotherapy.
- Positive result: pharmacologic EIF4A inhibition reduced osteosarcoma growth or lung colonization across several preclinical systems and blocked stress-induced NRF2 synthesis through translational control.
- Negative/uncertain result: the human outcome evidence is small/unadjusted; primary-tumor effects confound the spontaneous-metastasis experiment; the genetic intervention did not eliminate lung burden; global translation was not wholly unaffected; and clinical efficacy and safety were not tested.
- Claim ceiling: the source supports a preclinical EIF4A-NRF2 translation mechanism and anti-colonization rationale in osteosarcoma models. It does not establish clinical efficacy, prevention of human metastasis, an independently validated prognostic or predictive EIF4A1 biomarker, exclusive/necessary-and-sufficient NRF2 causality, or patient safety.
