### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/population: ARTEMIDE-01 was an open-label, multicenter, first-in-human phase I/II study of the bispecific PD-1/TIGIT antibody rilvegostomig. Part A was dose escalation (n=51; 70 mg n=4, 210 mg n=11, 750 mg n=22, 1,500 mg n=14), and Part B was a 750-mg expansion (n=32). The combined recommended-phase-II-dose population was n=54, and the full treated population was n=83. Participants had unresectable stage III or metastatic PD-L1-positive NSCLC previously treated with a checkpoint inhibitor; all 83 had prior checkpoint inhibition and 80/83 had prior platinum chemotherapy.
- Comparator and analysis boundary: there was no randomized or concurrent control. Analyses were descriptive and the paper states that no formal comparisons between doses or groups were planned. Part A was not powered for efficacy. Part B's size was selected for preliminary response precision and a low probability of declaring no targeted activity if the true response rate were 15%; this is not a comparative efficacy design.
- Analysis sets and denominators: safety used all 83 treated patients. Efficacy was reported for Part A (n=51), Part B (n=32), the pooled 750-mg/RP2D group (n=54), and all patients (n=83). Table 3 labels all 83 as response-evaluable while also listing two patients as not evaluable; therefore estimates are retained with the table's reported denominator and the non-evaluable count is not erased.
- Timing: tumor assessments were scheduled every 9 weeks through week 54 and every 18 weeks thereafter; safety follow-up extended to 90 days after the final dose. Duration-of-response and progression estimates must be labeled by their reported analysis group and cutoff rather than pooled across groups.
- Dose/PK/PD result: no dose-limiting toxicities occurred and the maximum tolerated dose was not reached. The 750-mg dose was selected as RP2D using the totality of safety, activity, pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and modeling information. Doses at or above 210 mg sustained at least 90% peripheral receptor occupancy; 750 mg was the lowest dose modeled to achieve at least 90% intratumoral occupancy. Intratumoral occupancy was modeled, not directly observed in tumor tissue, so it cannot be written as a measured tumor result.
- Safety, all treated n=83: 75 (90.4%) experienced a treatment-emergent adverse event; 24 (28.9%) had a maximum grade 3/4 TEAE; 28 (33.7%) had a serious adverse event; and 3 (3.6%) had an SAE with an outcome of death. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 45 (54.2%), grade 3/4 treatment-related events in 7 (8.4%), and treatment-related serious events in 6 (7.2%). There were no grade 4/5 treatment-related adverse events and no fatal SAE judged treatment related. Two treatment-related events led to discontinuation (grade 1 myocarditis and grade 3 acute hepatitis). Immune-mediated events occurred in 15 (18.1%), including grade 3 events in 4.8%, with none grade 4/5.
- Death classification boundary: the paper describes deaths and discontinuation events using more than one safety framing. Three fatal SAEs (pneumonia, brain metastases, and ileus) were judged unrelated, while hemoptysis and ileus also appear in the discontinuation/death narrative. The source does not support reclassifying the latter items beyond its own categories; the defensible statement is that no fatal event was considered treatment related, while all-cause fatal/discontinuation events remain separately reported.
- Immunogenicity: 2/83 had antidrug antibodies at baseline and 34/83 (41%) developed treatment-emergent antibodies. High titers were associated with an approximately 42% lower steady-state exposure. The study reports no apparent effect on safety or efficacy, but it was not designed or powered to exclude such an effect.
- Efficacy, Table 3: Part A ORR was 7.8% (95% CI, 2.2–18.9; four partial responses); Part B ORR was 3.1% (0.1–16.2; one partial response); RP2D ORR was 5.6% (1.2–15.4; three partial responses); and overall ORR was 6.0% (2.0–13.5; five partial responses). Overall, 33 had stable disease, 43 progressive disease, four death as best response, and two were not evaluable. These data indicate a low response fraction with broad uncertainty, not established efficacy.
- Durability and progression: median duration of response was 14.5 months (10.3–not calculable) in Part A, 4.1 months (not calculable–not calculable) in Part B, 10.3 months (4.1–not calculable) at RP2D, and 14.5 months (4.1–not calculable) overall. Six-month disease-control rates were 31.4%, 28.1%, 31.5%, and 30.1%, respectively. Median PFS was 3.1, 2.1, 3.8, and 2.1 months, respectively; 12-month PFS was 14.0%, 7.1%, 11.9%, and 11.5%. Overall and RP2D duration-of-response values are different denominated analyses, not a source contradiction; they must not be interchanged.
- Exploratory responder observation: four durable responders were all in the PD-L1 ≥50% subgroup, with reported duration-of-response/PFS pairs of 9.0/25.3, 14.5/16.4, 32.2/36.1, and 10.3/12.4 months. With only four patients, this is hypothesis-generating and does not establish PD-L1 as a predictive marker.
- Important baseline context: PD-L1 tumor proportion score was 1%–49% in 44 patients and ≥50% in 39. Primary and secondary checkpoint-inhibitor resistance were reported in 31 and 52 patients. Brain metastases differed descriptively between Part A (5/51, 9.8%) and Part B (13/32, 40.6%), without a formal between-part comparison.
- Positive result: feasibility of dosing, absence of DLTs, selection of 750 mg for further study, and responses including some durable responses in a heavily pretreated cohort.
- Negative/uncertain result: only five responses among 83 treated patients; median PFS was 2.1 months overall; all subgroup and dose contrasts were descriptive; overall survival was not an efficacy result here; receptor-occupancy and responder-subgroup inferences are uncertain.
- Claim ceiling: the source supports a tolerability description, an RP2D decision, and preliminary antitumor activity. It does not support superiority to standard care, an additive contribution of TIGIT blockade beyond PD-1 blockade, dose-comparative efficacy, efficacy in checkpoint-inhibitor-naïve disease, a validated predictive PD-L1 subgroup, or direct measurement of intratumoral target occupancy.

### Language and transfer

- Methods/statistical restraint (p. 13): the source explicitly frames statistics as descriptive and excludes formal dose/group comparisons. Reusable logic: state the analysis status before presenting a numerical contrast.
- Results/denominator partition (p. 18 and Table 3, pp. 40–42): efficacy is separated by Part A, Part B, RP2D, and overall populations. Reusable logic: attach every estimate to its exact set, especially when pooled and dose-specific values differ.
- Discussion/calibrated activity (pp. 21–23): activity is described as preliminary, with durable responses noted alongside the early-phase, single-arm limitations. Reusable logic: pair the observed signal and its strongest limitation in the same interpretive unit.
- Safe transfer template: `[Agent] showed [estimate] in this single-arm early-phase cohort; because analyses were descriptive and lacked a comparator, the finding supports preliminary activity rather than comparative efficacy.`
- Unsafe transfer: claiming causal benefit from dual PD-1/TIGIT blockade, comparing doses inferentially, extrapolating to checkpoint-inhibitor-naïve patients, describing modeled tumor occupancy as observed, or converting the four-patient responder pattern into a predictive biomarker claim.

### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/population: K-TAIL-202 was an open-label, multicenter, nonrandomized, single-arm phase II trial of first-line necitumumab plus pembrolizumab in Japanese patients with advanced NSCLC and PD-L1 tumor proportion score ≥50%. Necitumumab 800 mg was administered on days 1 and 8 and pembrolizumab 200 mg on day 1 of a 3-week cycle, for up to 35 cycles.
- Enrollment and analysis sets: 53 patients consented and 50 were enrolled; the efficacy and safety populations each comprised the 50 treated patients. Median age was 72 years; 38 were men. Histology was adenocarcinoma in 30, squamous in 15, and other in five. Disease status was stage IIIB n=1, IIIC n=2, IVA n=19, IVB n=18, and recurrent n=10.
- Comparator/statistical design: there was no concurrent pembrolizumab control. The primary endpoint was investigator-assessed ORR. The sample-size calculation used an expected ORR of 54.8% and a null threshold of 39.0%, one-sided alpha 0.10, and 80% power, yielding n=50. The null threshold derived from historical data and is not a randomized estimate of incremental benefit. Histology subgroup analyses were exploratory and no multiplicity adjustment was established.
- Timing: imaging occurred every 9 weeks through week 45 and every 12 weeks thereafter. Results reported a median follow-up of 14.2 months at the July 24, 2023 cutoff; the Discussion states 12.1 months. This internal follow-up discrepancy is retained rather than silently resolved, and any reused estimate should identify the source section/cutoff.
- Primary efficacy result: one complete response, 37 partial responses, five stable disease, four progressive disease, and three not evaluable were reported. ORR was 76.0% (95% CI, 61.9–86.9), with one-sided P<0.0001 against the prespecified 39% historical null; DCR was 86.0%. This establishes rejection of the study's historical null, not superiority or added benefit over pembrolizumab alone.
- Time-to-event outcomes: median PFS was 15.7 months (95% CI, 10.3–not reached), and median OS was not reached. At the reported maturity, OS cannot be converted into a survival-benefit claim. Two patients progressed with new brain metastases despite reductions in target lesions, showing why response plots and progression events should not be treated as interchangeable.
- Tumor-change display: 58% of patients had at least a 50% reduction in target lesions. The source's waterfall/trajectory plots visually support substantial target-lesion reduction in many patients, but the plot-label mismatch requires page-content tracing and does not change the RECIST endpoint definition.
- Treatment disposition: two patients completed 35 cycles and 18 remained on treatment; 30 discontinued, including 13 for progression, 13 for adverse events, three by patient request, and one by physician decision. This disposition must accompany “manageable” or “tolerable” language.
- Histology subgroup: among 15 patients with squamous histology, ORR was 93.3% (95% CI, 68.1–99.8) and median PFS 24.3 months (10.2–not reached); among 35 with nonsquamous histology, ORR was 68.6% (55.1–83.1) and median PFS 15.7 months (10.3–not reached). There was no formal powered interaction or adjusted comparison; the numerical difference is hypothesis-generating, not predictive.
- Safety denominator n=50: 98% had a treatment-related adverse event attributed to necitumumab, and 40% had grade ≥3 treatment-related events. Thirteen patients (26%) discontinued because of treatment-emergent adverse events. One grade 5 cardiac arrest occurred; its cause was undetermined, with no antecedent severe toxicity, and the patient was not transported for evaluation. It must not be relabeled treatment related or erased from the all-cause safety narrative.
- Interstitial lung disease: seven patients (14%) had ILD of any grade; five (10%) had grade 3 treatment-related ILD, with none grade 4/5. All improved to grade ≤1 after permanent discontinuation and corticosteroids. Enrollment was paused after the third grade 3 ILD, and the design's safety threshold allowed at most five such events; enrollment resumed and the total reached five. This material toxicity is required context for any safety conclusion.
- Other common toxicities: rash occurred in 64% (grade 3 in 10%), and hypomagnesemia in 60% (grade 3 in 4% and grade 4 in 4%), as displayed in Table 2.
- Positive result: ORR exceeded the prespecified historical threshold, with a median PFS of 15.7 months and substantial lesion reduction in many patients.
- Negative/uncertain result: the trial lacked a concurrent comparator; OS was immature/not reached; histology differences were exploratory; 26% discontinued for adverse events; grade 3 ILD affected 10%; and one grade 5 cardiac arrest of undetermined cause occurred. Mechanistic EGFR–PD-L1 synergy was not tested by translational data in this report.
- Claim ceiling: the source supports a promising single-arm activity signal in the defined Japanese PD-L1-high first-line population, alongside clinically important toxicity. It does not support superiority to pembrolizumab, attribution of added benefit to necitumumab, a causal EGFR–PD-L1 synergy claim, validated histology-based prediction, or routine-standard status.

## PMID 42456051

### Identity and coverage

- Title: *Spatial Configuration of Pancreatic Cancer Is Associated with Disease Recurrence after Neoadjuvant Therapy and Curative-Intent Resection*.
- Year: 2026.
- Supplied version: author manuscript.
- Registered PDF: `Cisneros_Spatial_Configuration_of_Pancreatic_Cancer_Is_Associated_with_Disease_Recurrence_after_Neoadju_PMID42456051.pdf`.

- Physical pages: 34/34 visually reviewed; no unreadable page.
- Supplement status: `not_supplied`. Supplement-referenced validation, feature, and workflow details were not inferred.
- Section map: title/abstract p. 1; abstract close/Introduction p. 2; Methods pp. 3–6; Results pp. 6–12; Discussion pp. 12–14; supplement notice/administrative material p. 15; abbreviations/references pp. 15–18; Translational Relevance p. 19; Figures 1–7 pp. 20–30, including caption continuations for Figure 2 on p. 22, Figure 4 on p. 25, Figure 5 on p. 27, and Figure 7 on p. 30; Table 1 pp. 31–32; Table 2 p. 33; Table 3 p. 34.
- Main-display coverage: all seven figures, all continued captions, and Tables 1–3 were visually checked against the Methods, Results, and Discussion.

### Evidence and result reconstruction

- Question/design/population: this was a retrospective, single-enterprise Mayo Clinic cohort of 203 patients with resected pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma after neoadjuvant therapy between 2010 and 2022. All had College of American Pathologists treatment response grade 2 or 3 and did not receive adjuvant therapy. Disease-free survival was measured from resection to recurrence or death. There were 123 recurrences and 80 recurrence-free observations at a mean follow-up of 18.9 months.
- Tissue/slide units: 297 whole-slide images were analyzed: 109 patients contributed one slide and 94 contributed two. The Methods say the most representative slides were selected from an average of five slides per case (range 1–16), whereas the Results describe one or two analyzed WSIs per patient. The source does not reconcile the candidate-slide pool with the final one/two-slide analytic set; reuse must preserve this selection-chain ambiguity rather than imply exhaustive tumor sampling.
- Computational method: PathExplore, a commercial image-analysis system, segmented tissue and generated 42 spatial features at patch, landscape, and patient levels. Only slides rated “Excellent” or “Good” were included. A reported lymphocyte intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.84 depends on unavailable supplementary detail and cannot be independently expanded from this PDF.
- Feature/model pipeline: correlations and univariable Cox models with spline assessment were followed by a P<0.20 screening threshold. Features were reduced through correlated clusters, in part by choosing the feature with the lowest Wilcoxon P value for recurrence, and then modeled using a recursive-partitioning regression tree (RPTR) and backward-selection Cox regression. Feature selection, model development, and performance assessment occurred in the same cohort; there was no held-out or external validation and no clearly established multiplicity correction. Reported C-indices of 0.632 (RPTR) and 0.644 (Cox) are modest internal estimates, not validated performance.
- Conventional-pathology and burden results: CAP grade 3 versus grade 2 showed a trend rather than conventional statistical significance (P=0.053). Cancer area was not associated with DFS (HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.97–1.31; P=0.13), and the cancer-to-stroma ratio was also not associated (HR 1.06, 0.91–1.23; P=0.45). These negative results motivate—but do not themselves validate—the spatial analyses.
- Spatial-response results: fragmentation, mixing, and within-tumor variability differed across pathologic-response categories in Figure 2, while several other spatial features did not (including stromal density P=0.28 and mean shape measures with P values up to 0.64). Diversity measures differed by response category but were not individually associated with DFS. Two unsupervised heatmap clusters were enriched for different CAP grades yet did not differ in DFS. These null results prevent a blanket statement that all spatial organization metrics were prognostic.
- RPTR result: the high-risk group (n=63) had median DFS 7.23 months versus 11.57 months in the low-risk group (n=140), with unadjusted HR 1.82 (95% CI, 1.31–2.52; P=0.0003). The adjusted HR was 1.71 with P=0.003. The source gives inconsistent adjusted confidence intervals: narrative text reports 1.17–2.63, whereas Table 3 reports 1.21–2.43. The point estimate and P value can be reported with a source-conflict flag; neither confidence interval should be silently selected as definitive.
- Cox-signature result: high-risk (n=51), intermediate-risk (n=101), and low-risk (n=51) groups had median DFS of 6.21, 9.37, and 20.91 months. The adjusted high-risk association was HR 2.19, P=0.002. Again, the adjusted confidence interval conflicts: narrative text reports 1.21–3.10 and Table 3 reports 1.34–3.58. Table 3's adjusted analysis includes n=193 and 148 events, rather than all 203 patients; the reduced multivariable denominator must accompany this result.
- Added cancer-area model: incorporating cancer area yielded a reported C-index of 0.666. This is an exploratory same-cohort increment and not evidence of externally validated improvement or clinical utility.
- Stage-stratified findings: Kaplan–Meier panels show separation within stage strata, with reported log-rank P values, but these analyses are not formal interaction tests. For the Cox signature, some contrasts are based on only the high- and low-risk extremes (approximately 101/102 patients), so they cannot establish stage-independent transportability.
- Immune/spatial correlations: the RPTR high-risk group had lower intratumoral lymphocyte ratio/density and higher stromal lymphocyte measures; peritumoral lymphocyte density was not different (P=0.819). Other correlations were variable, including nonsignificant mean cancer-shape associations with intratumoral lymphocyte ratio (Pearson r=0.066, P=0.35; Spearman r=0.12, P=0.078). These are spatial associations rather than a functional immune-exclusion mechanism.
- Positive result: two same-cohort spatial signatures were associated with DFS after covariate adjustment, and several architectural features captured information not reflected in gross residual cancer burden alone.
- Negative/uncertain result: conventional burden measures and several spatial features were null; the unsupervised clusters did not differ in DFS; performance was modest; multiple data-driven selection steps and absent validation increase optimism; confidence intervals conflict between narrative and Table 3; and causality/direction of immune–tumor organization is unresolved.
- Safety: no treatment-safety analysis was performed; this is a retrospective pathology/prognostic study.
- Claim ceiling: the paper supports candidate prognostic associations in this selected post-neoadjuvant resection cohort. It does not establish a validated biomarker, treatment prediction, adjuvant-treatment selection, clinical deployment, superiority to CAP grading in an external comparison, therapy-induced spatial remodeling, or a causal immune-exclusion mechanism. No paired pretreatment tissue or serial sampling establishes temporal change.

### Language and transfer

- Results/negative-to-exploratory pivot (around p. 7): residual cancer burden was not associated with DFS, after which the paper examines topology. Reusable logic: state the negative result completely before explaining why a different feature class was investigated.
- Discussion/causal restraint (p. 12): the source explicitly notes that whether spatial configuration is a cause or consequence of aggressive biology remains unresolved. Reusable logic: name competing causal directions rather than converting association into mechanism.
- Discussion/temporal limitation (p. 13): lack of serial sampling and the retrospective design constrain claims about treatment-induced change. Reusable logic: separate post-treatment association from longitudinal remodeling.
- Safe transfer template: `[Spatial signature] was associated with DFS after covariate adjustment, whereas [burden measure] was not; because feature selection and evaluation used the same cohort and no external validation was performed, the signature should be regarded as candidate prognostic rather than clinically validated.`
- Unsafe transfer: calling either signature validated or predictive of treatment benefit, recommending treatment selection, claiming causal immune exclusion or neoadjuvant remodeling, asserting externally proven superiority to CAP grading, or reporting either conflicting adjusted confidence interval without a source-inconsistency notice.

### Evidence and result reconstruction

Among MSS tumors, gallbladder cancer had higher reported median TMB than intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (4.1 vs 2.6; P=0.002) and higher fraction genome altered (0.495 vs 0.43; P=0.014). Whole-genome doubling was more frequent in extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (34.2%; P=0.002) and gallbladder cancer (32.0%; P=0.001) than intrahepatic disease (20.7%). Frequent MSS alterations included TP53 (36%), CDKN2A (21%), ARID1A (18%), KRAS (17%), and IDH1/2 (16%). These are subtype comparisons, not necessarily treatment-response biomarkers.
- Actionability denominator: the abstract reports an OncoKB-classified actionable alteration in 59%, while Table 1 partitions levels and shows 405/1,254 with level 1/2 alterations. For the clinical treatment analysis, 514 were considered potentially actionable using levels 1, 2, 3A, and selected 3B in Methods. Results later describes the eligible alterations as levels 1, 2, and 3A, omitting selected 3B. This category-definition inconsistency must be disclosed rather than harmonized without source support.
- Clinical eligibility flow: of 514 potentially actionable patients, 46 lacked survival data and 55 had incomplete follow-up, leaving 413 clinically eligible; 176 received matched targeted therapy and 237 did not. To reduce line-of-therapy/lead-time differences, the comparative outcome analysis further considered 100 treated patients who received matched therapy in the second line and 113 untreated patients who had received at least two systemic lines.
- PFS comparison and denominator: the reported second-line PFS comparison was between 99 patients receiving matched targeted therapy and 25 receiving second-line fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy, not all 100 versus 113 in the eligibility framing. Median PFS was 6.6 months (95% CI, 4.2–8.6) versus 3.3 months (2.1–8.1), with text P=0.005 and Figure 4 P=0.0052. The display-level difference is rounding; the more important limitation is the retrospective, selected, unadjusted comparison without randomized treatment assignment.
- OS comparison: median OS was 17.0 months (95% CI, 12–27) in the matched-therapy group versus 11.0 months (8.5–14) in unmatched patients, HR 0.8, P=0.16. This is statistically nonsignificant. Excluding immunotherapy yielded P=0.28, and restricting to level 1/2 alterations yielded P=0.34. The paper acknowledges lead-time, immortal-time, treatment-selection, and residual-confounding limitations; no causal survival benefit can be claimed.
- Molecular subgroups, Table 2: IDH1-altered disease had median PFS 4.2 months and treated versus untreated OS 13 versus 14 months (P=0.082). The source gives a mathematically incompatible CI for the treated median—median 13 months with CI 18.0–21.2—and this value is withheld as an unresolved source inconsistency rather than corrected. FGFR2-altered disease had median PFS 7.4 months and OS 27 versus 13 months (P=0.6). ERBB2-altered disease had median PFS 8.2 months and OS 12 versus 6.2 months (P=0.42). MSI-high/TMB-high disease had median PFS 22.5 months and OS 35 versus 19 months (P=0.55). None of these nonsignificant OS comparisons establishes survival benefit.
- BRAF subgroup: in a very small subgroup (n=16), median PFS was 16.6 months and treated versus untreated OS was 37 versus 9.5 months, HR 0.17, P=0.013. Given the tiny, selected, retrospective subgroup and multiple exploratory analyses, this is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.
- Co-alteration claims: narrative/abstract language suggests worse outcome with specific co-alterations, but adjusted estimates shown in Table 2 are nonsignificant: IDH1 with TP53/RAS HR 1.5 (95% CI, 0.9–2.7; P=0.149), FGFR2 with TP53 HR 1.1 (0.5–2.4; P=0.835), and ERBB2 with SMAD4 HR 0.74 (0.31–1.8; P=0.502), with the last point estimate not even in the stated adverse direction. Therefore the defensible wording is exploratory/univariable association, not an independent prognostic or predictive determinant.
- Sequential profiling: the text states that 21 MSS patients had paired/sequential profiling, yet the listed molecular groups sum to 22 (six IDH1/2, eight ERBB2, five FGFR2, two NTRK, and one BRAF) unless an undisclosed overlap exists; two additional MSI-high cases are described. This count inconsistency is explicit. In ERBB2 cases, the narrative separately mentions two mutation losses and three amplification losses, potentially overlapping, whereas the Discussion summarizes alteration loss in 4/8. These statements can coexist only if overlap is allowed; they must not be rewritten as five distinct patients. Reported acquired MET, MYC, KRAS, or MAPK-pathway events arise from very small case series and remain anecdotal resistance hypotheses.
- Positive result: broad sequencing identified actionable alterations in a substantial fraction, and receipt of matched therapy was associated with longer second-line PFS in a selected retrospective comparison. Selected small molecular subgroups showed long disease-control intervals.
- Negative/uncertain result: OS was not significantly different overall or in sensitivity restrictions; most subgroup OS contrasts were nonsignificant; adjusted co-alteration associations were nonsignificant; the BRAF signal was tiny and exploratory; actionability definitions, one subgroup CI, and sequential-case counts are internally inconsistent; and observational biases remain.
- Safety: no systematic treatment-safety or benefit–risk analysis was reported. Efficacy association must not be translated into a net clinical-benefit claim.
- Claim ceiling: the source supports molecular profiling as a way to identify targetable candidates and supports an observational association between matched therapy and second-line PFS. It does not establish a causal PFS or OS advantage, a survival benefit, independent adverse effects of the named co-alterations, validated resistance biomarkers, or generalizable benefit–risk. Sequential observations generate mechanisms for study rather than confirm them.

### Language and transfer

- Results/discordant endpoints (around p. 7 and Figure 4 p. 21): PFS was longer with matched therapy while OS was not significantly different. Reusable logic: report each endpoint's direction and uncertainty independently; do not allow a positive PFS result to overwrite a negative OS result.
- Discussion/bias limitation (p. 9): the paper explicitly considers lead-time and immortal-time biases. Reusable logic: name the observational mechanism that could create an apparent benefit, not merely “retrospective limitations.”
- Discussion/modest-subset framing (p. 10): targeted benefit is described as modest and concentrated in a subset. Reusable logic: distinguish molecular actionability from actual eligibility, receipt, and proven outcome improvement.
- Safe transfer template: `[Matched therapy] was associated with longer second-line PFS in this retrospective selected cohort, whereas OS did not differ significantly; residual treatment-selection, lead-time, and immortal-time bias preclude a causal survival-benefit claim.`
- Unsafe transfer: stating that sequencing-guided therapy improved survival causally, presenting the BRAF subgroup as confirmatory, calling the listed co-alterations independently adverse, resolving the impossible IDH1 confidence interval without an erratum, counting five distinct ERBB2-loss patients, or converting anecdotal acquired alterations into validated resistance biomarkers.
