## PMID 42440354 — Osimertinib Plus Gefitinib in Patients with EGFR-Mutated Advanced Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer and EGFR (C797X) Mutation Following First-Line Osimertinib: ORCHARD

**Identity and provenance.** Issue year 2026; supplied version `not_classified_by_header`; SHA-256 `118a92229dd085d634a41eeb6f660597dd3b353b6e6ff9e8660aaf216dda0112`; 10 physical pages. Supplement status is `not_supplied`; no supplement was reviewed.

**Coverage.** Abstract/Introduction: pp. 1–2; Patients and Methods: pp. 2–3; Results: pp. 3–5; Discussion: pp. 5–8; disclosures, references, and end matter: pp. 8–10. Main visuals checked with their captions: Fig. 1 (p. 4), Tables 1–2 (p. 5), Fig. 2 (p. 6), Fig. 3 (p. 7), and Table 3 (p. 8). All were legible.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** This was the final analysis of an open-label, multicenter, single-arm phase II ORCHARD module. Thirty-one patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC, progression on first-line osimertinib, and a postprogression EGFR C797S mutation received osimertinib 80 mg plus gefitinib 250 mg daily. All 31 treated eligible patients contributed to investigator-assessed RECIST 1.1 response, PFS, OS, and safety analyses. The primary endpoint was confirmed ORR; the protocol used a prespecified early-development decision framework with 80% exact-binomial CIs. Molecular analyses were exploratory and smaller: baseline/on-treatment ddPCR involved 11–12 patients, and paired baseline/postprogression ctDNA NGS involved 11.

**Conclusion-driving results.** Eight of 31 patients had confirmed partial responses, giving an ORR of 26% (80% CI, 16%–39%); 17 (55%) had stable disease for at least 6 weeks, and all responders later progressed or died. Median DoR was 4.2 months (95% CI, 2.8–5.5), median PFS 5.1 months (95% CI, 3.9–6.8; 30/31 events), and median OS 19.0 months (95% CI, 14.6–25.0; 19 deaths). The final ORR was lower than the interim 41% (7/17; 80% CI, 25%–59%), illustrating the instability of the early signal. In an exploratory, imbalanced subgroup, ORR was 32% (7/22) and median PFS 6.3 months for C797S clonality >20% without T790M versus 11% (1/9) and 3.8 months in all others; no confirmatory comparison was presented. Seven of 12 patients with baseline-detectable C797S ctDNA cleared it by cycle 3 day 1, but sensitizing-mutation ctDNA commonly persisted. Among 11 paired NGS cases, acquired T790M, BRAF, and PIK3CA/PTEN alterations supported heterogeneous resistance rather than a single mechanism.

**Safety and claim ceiling.** All 31 patients experienced a TEAE; 11 (35%) had grade 3 or higher TEAEs, five (16%) had serious AEs, and two (6%) discontinued both drugs because of TEAEs. There were no TEAE deaths; one grade 2 pneumonitis leading to discontinuation was considered possibly related to both drugs. The strongest defensible claim is that the regimen had modest single-arm activity with pharmacodynamic evidence of C797S suppression but limited durability and heterogeneous resistance. Neither the exploratory clonality comparison nor ctDNA clearance establishes treatment selection or prediction, and the authors concluded that the overall risk–benefit profile did not warrant further evaluation in this population.

**Source alerts and disposition.** Two patients were enrolled on local C797S testing that central NGS did not confirm; eligibility and central assay detection therefore must not be conflated. The clonality subgroups differed in age, prior-osimertinib duration, tumor burden, and CNS metastases, and their small sizes make the numerical outcome differences inconclusive. ORR uses an 80% CI whereas time-to-event outcomes use 95% CIs; these uncertainty levels must remain explicit. Separate supplementary methods and figures were unavailable and were not reviewed.

**Language samples and transfer.**

- Methods, p. 3, decision-rule move: the target and lower-reference ORR values are linked to an explicit early-development framework. Safe frame: “The sample size and continuation decision were based on prespecified [target] and [lower-reference] values, with [CI level] intervals used for preliminary efficacy assessment.”
- Results, p. 5, exploratory subgroup restraint: the article reports numerical ORR/PFS differences only after naming baseline imbalances and small samples. Safe frame: “[Outcome] was numerically higher in [subgroup] than in [comparator], but small numbers and baseline imbalances render the exploratory comparison inconclusive.”
- Discussion, pp. 7–8, target-engagement versus efficacy contrast: molecular suppression is set against persistent sensitizing ctDNA and acquired resistance. Safe frame: “Although [target signal] declined during treatment, persistence of [broader disease signal] and emergence of [resistance features] may help explain the limited clinical activity.”

Unsafe transfer: “C797S clearance predicts durable benefit from osimertinib plus gefitinib, particularly in tumors with high C797S clonality.” This converts small exploratory pharmacodynamic and subgroup observations into a validated predictive claim.

## PMID 42207168 — Mature Outcomes and Patterns of Failure in the Phase II FLARE-RT Trial of Biological Image-Guided and Risk-Adaptive Chemoradiation for Unresectable NSCLC

**Identity and provenance.** Issue year 2026; supplied version `author_manuscript`; SHA-256 `a59711a38d6f57596977129879c2c41774b405a5a0b473b42114fb99d423cec5`; 21 physical pages. Supplement status is `not_supplied`; the main manuscript refers to supplementary tables/figures, but none was supplied or reviewed.

**Coverage.** Abstract/Introduction: pp. 1–3; Patients and Methods: pp. 3–6; Results: pp. 6–9; Discussion: pp. 9–11; references and end matter: pp. 12–14; figures: pp. 15–17; tables: pp. 18–21. Main visuals checked with captions: Figs. 1–3 (pp. 15–17), Table 1 (pp. 18–19), Table 2 (p. 20), and Table 3 (p. 21). Every physical page and all main panels were visually reviewed and legible.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** FLARE-RT (NCT02773238) was a single-institution, nonrandomized phase II trial with prospective risk stratification. Forty-nine patients with AJCC version 7 stage IIB–IIIB NSCLC or locoregional recurrence enrolled during 2016–2021 and initiated concurrent platinum-doublet chemoradiation. Week-3 FDG-PET response was assigned by multifactorial imaging criteria and multidisciplinary consensus. Thirty-three PET responders continued 60 Gy in 30 fractions; 16 PET nonresponders were assigned a concomitant, uptake-scaled boost to 74 Gy, with peak tumor doses exceeding 90 Gy. Consolidation therapy evolved during the trial: 25 received durvalumab, four another immunotherapy, and 20 none. OS was primary; PFS, competing-risk locoregional progression (LRP) and distant metastasis (DM), adverse events, and exploratory imaging/dosimetric models were reported. Twenty-six candidate features were screened without multiplicity adjustment; selected Fine–Gray models and five-fold internal cross-validation were not externally validated.

**Mature outcomes and failure patterns.** At a median follow-up of 52.3 months, one- and two-year OS were 81.6% (95% CI, 71.5%–93.2%) and 54.2% (41.8%–70.4%), and median OS was 29.1 months (17.3–not estimable). One- and two-year PFS were 53.1% (40.9%–69.0%) and 40.5% (28.8%–57.0%), and median PFS was 12.3 months (9.2–not estimable). One- and two-year cumulative LRP were 12.2% (4.9%–23.2%) and 18.5% (9.0%–30.6%); corresponding DM estimates were 34.7% (21.7%–48.1%) and 43.1% (28.9%–56.5%). Thirty-one patients had failure or death; DM occurred in 18 and was the predominant failure mode, while nine had LRP. These single-arm estimates may be described as mature cohort outcomes, but historical comparisons with RTOG or PACIFIC cannot establish equivalence or treatment benefit.

**Imaging and treatment-associated results.** Higher baseline total lesion glycolysis (pre-TLG) was associated with LRP in univariable analysis (HR per 1-SD increase 1.87, 95% CI 1.41–2.49; `P<.001`), and higher midtreatment TLG was associated with DM (HR 1.52, 1.05–2.21; `P=.03`). Durvalumab receipt was associated with lower DM (HR 0.40, 0.18–0.92; `P=.03`) but was not randomized. Two-variable models yielded HR 2.97 (1.83–4.82) for pre-TLG and 11.67 (1.80–75.85) for adenocarcinoma in the LRP model (C-index 0.82, 0.68–0.95), and HR 0.38 (0.17–0.86) for durvalumab plus HR 1.54 (1.15–2.06) for mid-TLG in the DM model (C-index 0.66, 0.53–0.77). In internally cross-validated strata, pre-TLG alone did not significantly separate one-year LRP (7.7% vs 17.4%; `P=.056`), whereas mid-TLG separated one-year DM (25.0% vs 47.6%; `P=.027`). Combined scores separated LRP (0% vs 24.0%; `P=.012`) and DM (20.0% vs 50.0%; `P=.011`) within this same small cohort. A data-derived pre-TLG cutoff of 371 g had a two-year AUC of 0.768 and classified 16/49 above threshold; it is not a validated clinical decision threshold. No stable optimal mid-TLG threshold was found for DM.

PET nonresponders assigned boost had numerically lower one-year LRP than PET responders receiving standard dose (6.3% vs 18.2%; HR 0.23), but the difference was nonsignificant. Because response status determined dose and there was no standard-dose nonresponder group, this comparison cannot estimate the causal effect of boosting. Likewise, the observed durvalumab association is vulnerable to treatment-era and clinical-selection confounding and does not replicate the randomized PACIFIC effect within this cohort.

**Safety and claim ceiling.** Across all 49 patients, grade 2 or higher dermatitis, pneumonitis, and esophagitis occurred in 28.6%, 40.8%, and 59.2%, respectively. In nonresponders receiving boost versus responders receiving 60 Gy, grade 2 or higher pneumonitis was 31.2% versus 45.5% and dermatitis 18.8% versus 33.3%, whereas esophagitis was 81.3% versus 48.5%. No grade 3 or higher esophagitis occurred in boosted nonresponders, versus one event in responders; the Discussion reports grade 3 or higher pneumonitis in 8.2% overall. The complete adverse-event table was supplementary and unavailable, so “safe” or “no excess toxicity” is stronger than the reviewed evidence. The strongest defensible conclusion is that this small nonrandomized strategy produced mature, descriptively favorable LRP estimates and generated internally cross-validated imaging-risk hypotheses. It does not show that adaptive boosting improves control, that TLG should guide intensification, or that toxicity is equivalent.

**Source alerts and disposition.** The abstract reports one-year DM of 20.0% versus 54.2% for durvalumab versus no durvalumab (`P=.04`), whereas Results report 20.0% versus 50.0% (`P=.032`); the body/figure-supported values are retained and the abstract values withheld. Methods describe a boost-plan predictor subset of 15 patients even though allocation and Table 2 describe 16 nonresponders receiving boost; the smaller analysis set is retained without assuming why one patient was absent. On p. 8, a sentence describing equivocal DM rates labels the 30.8% versus 39.1% values “one-year LRP”; the mislabeled values are not transferred. The manuscript screens 26 features with unadjusted univariable p values, has only nine LRP events, derives cutoffs and tests models internally, and supplies no independent validation. Supplement-only adverse-event, representativeness, full predictor, and failure-pattern data were not available.

**Language samples and transfer.**

- Methods, pp. 5–6, exploratory-model transparency: feature screening, lack of multiplicity adjustment, event-limited model size, and internal cross-validation are stated in sequence. Safe frame: “Candidate features were screened in exploratory analyses without multiplicity adjustment; the event-limited multivariable model was assessed by internal cross-validation.”
- Results, pp. 7–8, nonsignificant signal restraint: the lower LRP estimate in boosted nonresponders is explicitly described as a trend. Safe frame: “[Outcome] was numerically lower in [risk-adapted group] than in [comparison group], but the difference was not statistically significant and the groups differed by the factor that determined treatment.”
- Discussion, pp. 9–11, favorable local outcome versus null survival/historical limits: the paper contrasts low LRP with no OS improvement and heterogeneous systemic therapy. Safe frame: “Although locoregional-control estimates were encouraging, survival was not improved relative to historical cohorts and distant failure remained predominant; cross-trial differences preclude comparative efficacy inference.”
Unsafe transfer: “Week-3 PET-guided boosting improves locoregional control without additional toxicity, and a pre-TLG threshold of 371 g should determine dose escalation.” Neither a randomized boost comparison nor external threshold validation was performed, and full safety data were unavailable.

## PMID 42405849 — Presenting features and outcomes to standard systemic therapies in patients with MTAP-deleted advanced non-small cell lung cancer

**Identity and provenance.** Issue year 2026; supplied version `author_manuscript`; SHA-256 `479a9cbb871a3396c14f060e01055ffa123791276a20ea26ffb5051535030e69`; 21 physical pages. Supplement status is `not_recorded`; the manuscript points to supplementary data, but no separate supplement was supplied or reviewed.

**Coverage.** Abstract/Introduction: pp. 2–3; Materials and Methods: pp. 3–5; Results: pp. 5–8; Discussion: pp. 9–11; references and end matter: pp. 11–15; main figures: pp. 16–19; Table 1: pp. 20–21. Main visuals checked with captions: Fig. 1 (p. 16), Fig. 2 (pp. 17–18), Fig. 3 (p. 19), and Table 1 (pp. 20–21). Every physical page and all main panels were visually reviewed and legible.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** This was a single-center retrospective cohort of patients with advanced NSCLC who underwent tissue-based OncoPanel sequencing and received at least one systemic regimen of interest during October 2016–March 2024. MTAP deletion meant homozygous/two-copy loss; MTAP wild type meant both copies intact, and one-copy deletions were excluded. The final cohort contained 93 MTAP-deleted and 307 MTAP-wild-type patients. To reduce false-negative wild-type calls, tumor purity had to be at least 40% for MTAP-wild-type cases, whereas MTAP-deleted cases could have purity at least 20%, or lower purity with both NGS deletion and IHC-confirmed protein loss. Regimen-specific cohorts overlapped because patients could receive sequential platinum doublet, anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy, platinum plus anti-PD-(L)1, and docetaxel-based therapy. PFS was based on clinically documented progression, new metastasis in scan reports, or death rather than centrally adjudicated RECIST. Prespecified Cox covariates were histology, age, smoking pack-years, PD-L1 category, targetable co-mutation group, and treatment line.

**Clinical and genomic presentation.** Relative to the 307 MTAP-wild-type patients, the 93 MTAP-deleted patients more often were female (65.6% vs 51.5%; `P=.018`), presented with stage IV disease (78.8% vs 60.9%; `P=.002`) and brain metastases (43.0% vs 26.1%; `P=.003`), and less often had at least 30 smoking pack-years (33.3% vs 49.0%; `P=.008`). They more often had PD-L1 TPS below 1% (37.9% vs 24.3%; `P=.017`) and had lower median TMB (7.6 vs 9.9 mutations/Mb; `P=.012`), while age, race/ethnicity, histology, other metastatic sites, and ECOG performance status did not significantly differ. These are presenting associations, not proof that MTAP loss itself causes biologically aggressive disease.

MTAP deletion was strongly associated with two-copy CDKN2A (91.4% vs 4.2%; `P<.001`) and CDKN2B loss (89.2% vs 3.9%; `P<.001`). Only 11/84 evaluable MTAP-deleted tumors (13.1%) had copy-number loss extending to assessed adjacent 9p loci. Any targetable alteration was numerically more frequent (41.9% vs 30.9%; `P=.060`) but not significant. Clinically actionable EGFR alterations were enriched (21.5% vs 8.5%; `P=.001`), particularly EGFR L858R (10.8% vs 3.3%; `P=.011`); in the 30-gene analysis, overall EGFR alteration enrichment remained after Bonferroni correction (39.8% vs 20.8%; `q<.030`). These co-alterations complicate attribution of phenotype or treatment outcome to MTAP itself.

**Regimen-specific positive and null results.** On anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy, unadjusted median PFS was 2.0 versus 4.9 months for MTAP-deleted versus wild-type disease (n=28 vs 128; HR 2.0, 95% CI 1.2–3.3; `P=.003`); the adjusted association remained (HR 1.70, 1.02–2.82; `P=.040`). On platinum doublet plus anti-PD-(L)1, median PFS was 4.2 versus 7.5 months (n=40 vs 104; unadjusted HR 2.0, 1.4–3.3; `P<.001`; adjusted HR 1.89, 1.20–2.97; `P=.006`). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to tumor purity at least 40%, adjusted PFS associations persisted for combination therapy (HR 2.18, 1.33–3.51; `P=.002`) and were borderline for monotherapy (HR 1.74, 1.00–2.90; `P=.050`).

By contrast, neither PFS nor OS differed significantly with platinum doublet alone (PFS 4.8 vs 5.4 months; HR 1.4, 0.9–2.0; `P=.108`; OS 11.9 vs 17.4 months; HR 1.6, 1.0–2.5; `P=.054`) or docetaxel-based therapy (PFS 1.6 vs 2.0 months; HR 1.2, 0.6–2.5; `P=.622`; OS 4.5 vs 6.4 months; HR 1.3, 0.6–3.3; `P=.474`), although only nine MTAP-deleted patients received docetaxel. OS was also nonsignificant after anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy (12.7 vs 16.7 months; HR 1.2, 0.8–2.0; `P=.367`) and chemoimmunotherapy (14.9 vs 19.1 months; HR 1.3, 0.8–2.0; `P=.194`). Thus, the central pattern is shorter observed PFS but not OS in the two ICI-containing cohorts—not a proven predictive interaction with ICI.

**Safety, mechanism, and claim ceiling.** Treatment safety was not evaluated. Mechanistic explanations involving MTA-mediated T-cell suppression, CDKN2A/B loss, interferon-gene co-deletion, or broader 9p loss were literature-informed hypotheses; the assay did not specifically sequence the interferon cluster, and the study did not measure immune function. The strongest defensible conclusion is that, in this selected retrospective cohort, MTAP two-copy loss co-occurred with distinct clinical/genomic features and remained associated with shorter PFS during two anti-PD-(L)1-containing regimens after the reported adjustment. Because treatment was not randomized, regimen cohorts overlapped, no formal treatment-by-biomarker interaction was tested, and important residual differences such as stage, brain metastases, TMB, sex, and performance status were not all included in the reported adjustment, the study cannot establish MTAP as a predictive ICI-resistance biomarker or support withholding immunotherapy.

**Source alerts and disposition.** Fig. 1A labels the excluded 350 one-copy-deletion cases as “MTAPwt patients with MTAP one-copy deletion,” which conflicts with the Methods definition of MTAP wild type as both copies intact; they are treated only as an excluded one-copy group, not as wild type. The purity threshold was asymmetric between cohorts, although the ≥40% sensitivity analysis preserved direction. The treatment cohorts are nonexclusive, making OS comparisons across regimens nonindependent. Supplement-only baseline tables, multivariable model coefficients/diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses were not independently checked. The authors' phrases “aggressive subset,” “worse outcomes to anti-PD-(L)1,” and “regardless of PD-L1 TPS” should be transferred as retrospective associations, not causal biology or proof of treatment-specific prediction.

**Language samples and transfer.**

- Methods, pp. 3–5, assay-misclassification transparency: distinct purity rules are justified and followed by a symmetric sensitivity analysis. Safe frame: “To reduce false-negative [wild-type] calls, [purity criterion] was applied to that group; because the primary cohorts used asymmetric thresholds, analyses were repeated after applying [common criterion] to both groups.”
- Results, pp. 7–8, positive and null endpoint pairing: shorter PFS is reported alongside null OS and null results for other regimens. Safe frame: “[Biomarker] was associated with shorter PFS during [regimen] after adjustment, whereas OS did not differ significantly; neither endpoint differed significantly in [comparison regimen], for which precision was limited.”
- Discussion, p. 9, endpoint-discordance interpretation: numerical OS differences are not upgraded when p values are nonsignificant. Safe frame: “Although median OS was numerically lower, the comparison was not statistically significant; subsequent-treatment heterogeneity may partly explain the divergence from PFS.”
Unsafe transfer: “MTAP deletion predicts resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors, so MTAP-deleted patients should avoid anti-PD-(L)1 therapy.” The study did not test a treatment interaction, eliminate residual confounding, assess comparative treatment benefit, or evaluate a de-escalation strategy.

## PMID 42440361 — Divergent Tumor Immune Microenvironment and Response to Neoadjuvant Chemoimmunotherapy in Resectable Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer: A Single-Arm Phase II Trial

**Identity and provenance.** Issue year 2026; supplied version `not_classified_by_header`; SHA-256 `1fd990e640ed70962f9aa5d382afad178bb0bf94dfa9f85f64f43a2af1005285`; 13 physical pages. Supplement status is `not_supplied`; no supplement was reviewed.

**Coverage.** Abstract/Introduction: pp. 1–2; Patients and Methods: pp. 2–4; Results: pp. 4–7; Discussion: pp. 8–11; disclosures, references, and end matter: pp. 11–13. Main visuals checked with captions: Fig. 1 (p. 5), Tables 1–2 (p. 6), Table 3 (p. 7), Fig. 2 (p. 8), Fig. 3 (p. 9), and Fig. 4 (p. 10). Every physical page and all main panels were visually reviewed and legible.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** This was a single-center, single-arm phase II trial (NCT05383716) in 80 Chinese patients with resectable NSCLC. Patients received two to four cycles of pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy before surgery, followed by postoperative pembrolizumab for up to one year, with chemotherapy permitted. Major pathologic response (MPR) was primary; pathologic complete response, event-free survival (EFS), overall survival, radiographic response, and safety were secondary. Fifty-five patients underwent surgery; 25 did not, including 17 judged unfit, five who declined, and three with adverse events. The spatial discovery analysis selected 11 MPR and 11 non-MPR surgical cases and profiled 124 cores/127 regions of interest with an 1,812-gene panel. Whole-transcriptome validation used the remaining 26 tumors (15 MPR and 11 non-MPR; 52 cores/84 regions). External assessments used a 21-patient pretreatment neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy cohort and four small advanced-disease ICI-monotherapy cohorts; these settings are not clinically interchangeable.

**Clinical results.** The paper reports MPR in 30 patients (37.5%) and pathologic complete response in 21 (26.3%); both percentages use all 80 enrolled patients as denominator, although pathologic response was observed among the 55 operated patients. At 24 months, EFS was 71.9% (95% CI, 62.6%–82.6%); median EFS was not reached (95% CI, 42.1 months–not reached), and median OS was not reached. MPR was associated with longer EFS than non-MPR (HR 0.33, 95% CI 0.12–0.91), but this posttreatment-defined, surgery-selected comparison is prognostic/response-associated rather than evidence that MPR predicts benefit from pembrolizumab. Among surgical patients, PD-L1 expression was associated with MPR (`P=.009`). With no comparator arm, the study cannot isolate the contribution of immunotherapy or establish superiority over chemotherapy.

**Omics and validation results.** Posttreatment MPR tumors showed greater immune-activation, T-cell, and B-cell signals, whereas non-MPR tumors showed stress, immunosuppression, and epithelial–mesenchymal-transition programs. Twenty-three derived signatures were tested in the small external pretreatment neoadjuvant cohort, with reported AUCs of 0.778–0.852; this cross-cohort association is promising but does not validate treatment-specific prediction. Spatial analysis associated MPR with higher plasma-cell, CD8 T-cell, B-cell, and T-follicular-helper signals and lower endothelial-cell and NK-cell signals, while no immune subset differed significantly in draining lymph nodes (`P>.05`). Among only 15 MPR cases in the internal validation set, a higher mesenchymal-enriched signature showed a nonsignificant trend toward shorter EFS (`P=.094`), whereas a higher lymphocyte-enriched signature was associated with longer EFS (`P=.041`). Associations in advanced ICI-treated cohorts were directionally supportive, including shorter OS for the mesenchymal-enriched signature in one cohort (`P=.007`), longer survival for the lymphocyte-enriched signature in two cohorts (`P=.022` and `.023`), and enrichment among responders in two cohorts (`P=.047` and `.003`). None of these analyses includes an untreated comparison or a treatment interaction; they therefore support association, not an ICI-benefit biomarker.

**Safety and claim ceiling.** During neoadjuvant treatment, 49/80 patients (61.3%) had a treatment-related adverse event, 21/80 (26.3%) had a grade 3–4 event, 20/80 (25.0%) had an immune-related event, and 5/80 (6.3%) had a grade 3–4 immune-related event. One patient died from immune-related myocarditis. Perioperative complications occurred in 8/55 operated patients (14.5%). The strongest defensible conclusion is that the regimen produced pathologic responses in this single-arm cohort and that posttreatment spatial states were associated with pathologic response and subsequent outcomes. The single posttreatment time point cannot establish treatment-induced remodeling, and the signatures cannot yet guide adjuvant treatment.

**Source alerts and disposition.** The MPR and pathologic-complete-response percentages are arithmetically 30/80 and 21/80, despite the text also identifying 55 operated, pathology-evaluable patients; raw counts and the authors' enrolled-cohort percentages are retained, but they must not be relabeled as percentages among surgical patients. The title and conclusion use causal or predictive language about divergent treatment response and adjuvant benefit that exceeds the single-arm, posttreatment design. Regions of interest were selected to enrich immune-containing areas, patient numbers were small, and ROI-level observations do not create independent patient-level replication. Most external cohorts consisted of advanced disease treated with ICI monotherapy, not perioperative or adjuvant therapy. Separate supplementary material was unavailable.

**Language samples and transfer.**

- Methods, pp. 3–4, discovery/validation separation: posttreatment spatial profiling is explicitly divided into a selected discovery set and a nonoverlapping internal validation set. Safe frame: “Posttreatment spatial profiling was performed in [selected discovery cohort], and the resulting signatures were assessed in [nonoverlapping internal set] and [external cohorts].”
- Results, pp. 9–10, positive/nonsignificant contrast: one signature is associated with EFS while another yields only a trend. Safe frame: “Higher [signature A] was associated with [endpoint], whereas [signature B] showed only a nonsignificant trend (`P=[value]`).”
- Discussion, pp. 10–11, transfer restraint: cross-cohort consistency is used to motivate prospective study rather than clinical deployment. Safe frame: “Associations across treated cohorts support prospective validation, but do not establish treatment-specific benefit or clinical utility.”
Unsafe transfer: “The lymphocyte-enriched 18-gene signature predicts benefit from adjuvant pembrolizumab and should guide postoperative therapy.” No untreated interaction, randomized comparison, or prospective decision-impact analysis supports that claim.

## PMID 42440358 — Application of a Translational Research Platform to Unveil Efficacy Signals and Mechanisms of Resistance of FGFR Inhibitors in Multiple FGFR-Altered Solid Tumors

**Identity and provenance.** Issue year 2026; supplied version `publisher_typeset_pdf`; SHA-256 `03a356ebcc6c02232e55367da294acb359b60203032072c0e4fba9dd639f187b`; 15 physical pages. Supplement status is `not_supplied`; no supplement was reviewed.

**Coverage.** Abstract/Introduction: pp. 1–4; Materials and Methods: pp. 4–6; Results: pp. 6–9; Discussion: pp. 9–12; disclosures, references, and end matter: pp. 12–15. Main visuals checked with captions: Fig. 1 (p. 2), Fig. 2 (pp. 3–4), Fig. 3 (pp. 5–6), Fig. 4 (pp. 7–8), Fig. 5 (p. 10), and Fig. 6 (p. 11). There were no main tables. Every physical page and all main panels were visually reviewed and legible.

**Question, design, and analysis sets.** This was an ambispective proof-of-principle translational platform rather than a therapeutic trial. A retrospective series included 36 patients with FGFR-altered solid tumors treated on phase I studies during 2011–2016; two patients received two treatments, producing 38 treated cases. Archival/new biopsies, autopsy samples, patient-derived xenografts (PDX), genomics, RNA, proteomics, and a functional annotation for cancer treatment (FACT) reporter assay were variably available. The tumor types, drugs, assay subsets, and sampling times were heterogeneous.
